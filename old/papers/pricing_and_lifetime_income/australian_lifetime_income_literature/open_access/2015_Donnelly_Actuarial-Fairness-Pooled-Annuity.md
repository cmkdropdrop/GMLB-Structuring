It is simply a rule to\r\nallocate the group’s funds among the participants. However, the AOF and the GSA approach\r\nthe sharing of mortality risk differently. They have different aims. In the GSA, the approach is\r\na group risk-sharing perspective and in the AOF it is an individual member perspective.\r\nThe GSA assumes that the actual number of deaths in the scheme should be the expected\r\nnumber of deaths, assuming no longevity risk, since this is the basis of the calculation of the\r\nfuture consumption payments. This is reasonable when there are enough members to sufficiently\r\npool mortality risk, by the Law of Large Numbers. In the GSA, deviations of the observed\r\nnumber of deaths from the expected number of deaths result in adjustments to the consumption\r\npayments. For example, if deaths turn out exactly as expected for Group A but not as expected\nActuarial fairness and solidarity in pooled annuity funds 14\r\nfor Group B, then both Group A and B members will have their consumption payments adjusted\r\nby the same percentage.\r\nThe AOF starts with the requirement that the fund is actuarially fair for each member,\r\nby taking into account the actual scheme membership profile in the calculation of the actuarial\r\ngains due to mortality risk-sharing. Actuarial gains due to deaths in the AOF result in members’\r\nfund values changing by different percentages, according to their individual mortality rate and\r\nwealth, rather than by the same percentage as in the GSA.\r\nThe structure of the GSA is focused on paying its members a consumption stream. Members\r\npay an initial contribution and do not individually own a share of the assets of the GSA. This\r\nmeans that individuals give up their claim on their initial contribution in exchange for the\r\nconsumption stream.\r\nIn contrast, the AOF emphasizes an investment frame, in which the result of mortality risk\u0002sharing is an additional, nonnegative return on an individual’s assets. Participants in an AOF\r\nretain investment control over their own assets. Indeed, theoretically these assets could include\r\nthe individual’s house which would only need to be sold upon the death of the individual to\r\norder to share its financial value among the AOF members; in the meantime the individual\r\ncould continue living in their house. This is unlike the GSA which, as it pays an income stream\r\nto its members, could not have its participants continuing to own and live in their own houses.\r\nThe GSA requires the initial contributions upfront, whereas the AOF requires it upon the death\r\nof the member.\r\nAs the mortality risk-sharing rule in the AOF results in a payment to the estate of the\r\nrecently deceased, there is less money to be shared among the survivors.
